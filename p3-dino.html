DINO

Two networks that learn to agree about pictures without a single label. This part builds DINO one piece at a time: the student and the teacher, the crops each one sees, the head and its prototypes, centring and sharpening, the loss over the pairs of crops, the teacher that is an average of the student's past, and the recipe. Every equation gets a walk with numbers you can check by hand, every decision a why card, and the last section shows what three real models make of a real photograph.Toy

3.1 Two networks that agree without labels

Assumes: Part 1 (a loss, a gradient step, softmax with a temperature) and Part 2 (a crop becomes tokens). Gives: what DINO trains, why it needs a student and a teacher, what collapse is, and how the papers score the features afterwards.

Training changes a network's weights until its outputs become useful. Show it a batch of pictures, measure how wrong its outputs are with one number, the loss, compute the gradient and move every weight a small step downhill (Part 1). One such round is a step, and one pass over all the training pictures is an epoch. In supervised learning the loss compares each output with a label, such as the word 'dog' written under a photograph.Toy

DINO has no label anywhere. It trains on the 1,281,167 photographs of ImageNet without using their labels, and its authors call it DINO and describe it as a form of self-distillation with no labels.Fact

Distillation trains a student network to reproduce the outputs of a teacher network. In DINO the two networks share one architecture and differ only in their weights. Only the student learns from the loss. The teacher is built from the student's own past, as an exponential moving average of the student's weights, and at step 0 it is an exact copy of the student. A teacher made from the student itself is why the method is called self-distillation.Fact

Why two networks? Picture one network that must give the same answer for two crops of one picture and that learns from its own answer. The cheapest way to agree needs no looking at all: give every picture the same answer, and the two crops of any picture always agree. That failure is called collapse. A teacher that moves slowly gives the student a target that is more than its own current guess, and two operations on the teacher's side, centring and sharpening (3.4), close the two lazy exits.Toy

DINO's supplement names the two forms of collapse: whatever the input, the output is uniform along all its dimensions, or one dimension dominates it.Fact

The authors report that copying the student's weights into the teacher fails to converge, while freezing the teacher for an epoch works surprisingly well. They describe the moving average as a form of model ensembling, in which several models answer together, similar to Polyak-Ruppert averaging with an exponential decay, an average of the weights over the steps of training in which older steps count less and less, and they observe that this teacher performs better than the student throughout training, so it gives the student targets of higher quality.Fact

If the teacher comes from the student, where does anything new come from? From the views. The student must give, for one crop of a picture, the answer the teacher gives for another crop of the same picture. Describing what the picture shows is one way to do that, and centring and sharpening block the ways that need no description.Toy

One step of training, in five moves, as DINO's Algorithm 1 and its multi-crop rule run it:Fact

  1. cut each picture of the batch into crops and change each crop at random (3.2);
  2. pass every crop through the student and the two large crops through the teacher; each network ends in a head that scores the crop against many prototypes (3.3);
  3. turn the scores into probabilities: the teacher's centred and sharpened, the student's softer (3.4);
  4. measure the loss, the cross-entropy over the pairs of crops, and move the student's weights against its gradient (3.5);
  5. move the teacher's weights a little towards the student's, and the centre a little towards the teacher's latest average (3.6).

The why cards quote one yardstick again and again, so here it is first. How do the papers tell good features from bad ones? They freeze the trained network, so that its features no longer change, and only then bring in ImageNet's labels, to measure. A k-NN classifier stores the features of the labelled training pictures and gives a new picture the label chosen by a weighted vote of the k stored features nearest to its own; DINO lets the 20 nearest vote, the number that worked best in most of its runs. A linear classifier is one linear layer fitted with the labels on top of the frozen features. The accuracy is the share of ImageNet's validation pictures whose right class comes out first: for DINO's ViT-S/16, 74.5 % with k-NN and 77.0 % with a linear classifier. The labels judge the features after training and never enter it.Fact

A vote worked by hand, with k = 3: if the three stored pictures nearest to a new one are two dogs and a cat, the k-NN classifier answers 'dog'. The figure draws it in two dimensions, with a straight boundary beside it, the kind a linear classifier fits.Toy

The cards also quote DINO's Table 7, which compares training choices on ViT-S/16 after 300 epochs; there DINO's own row reads 72.8 % k-NN and 76.1 % linear accuracy. The 74.5 % and 77.0 % above belong to the released ViT-S/16, whose run was configured for 800 epochs.Fact

The same table trains three other self-supervised methods on the same ViT-S/16, and several cards compare DINO with them. MoCo-v2 has a momentum encoder and a contrastive loss, InfoNCE, that compares each image with other images. BYOL trains its features to match a momentum encoder's outputs too, with a predictor on the student and a mean squared error as its loss. SwAV has no momentum teacher and uses an algorithm called Sinkhorn-Knopp on its prototypes; DINO's head is inspired by SwAV's prototype layer (3.3).Fact

Decision

Train a student network to match a teacher network that has the same architecture and is built from the student's own past: self-distillation, with no label anywhere.

Why

The authors cast self-supervised learning as knowledge distillation in which no teacher exists in advance, so they build the teacher from past iterations of the student, and they call the result self-distillation with no labels.Fact

Evidence

Without the momentum teacher, DINO's framework does not work: row 2 of Table 7 reaches 0.1 % k-NN and 0.1 % linear accuracy against 72.8 % and 76.1 % for DINO, both measured afterwards with ImageNet's labels.Fact

What it does to training

At every step the gradient flows into the student only; the teacher's answers are fixed targets for that step, and the teacher moves afterwards, by averaging the student's weights.Fact

If you changed it

A teacher that is a copy of the student, or the student of the previous iteration, fails at 0.1 % k-NN accuracy; the student of the previous epoch works, at 66.6 % against 72.8 % for the moving average.Fact

3.2 Many views of one picture: multi-crop

Assumes: 3.1 and the patches of Part 2. Gives: the crops and their changes, who sees which crop, and why small crops answer for large ones.

Each image becomes several views, which are random crops. In SelfPatch's recipe, which our runs use, each image gives 2 large global crops and 8 small local crops per step, 10 in all. A global crop covers 0.25 to 1.0 of the image's area and is resized to 224 × 224 pixels; a local crop covers 0.05 to 0.25 and is resized to 96 × 96, both with bicubic interpolation (2.2). Mixing large and small crops in this way is called multi-crop.Fact

The released DINO ViT-S/16 used 10 local crops, so its student saw 12 crops of every image. DINO's code defaults differ again (global crops of 0.4 to 1.0 of the area, local crops of 0.05 to 0.4, 8 local crops), and the paper describes global views as covering, for example, more than 50 % of the image and local views less than 50 %.Fact

Each crop is also changed at random. These changes are augmentations, and they make two views differ in more than their framing. The recipe of the official code:Fact

  • mirror left to right with probability 0.5;
  • colour jitter with probability 0.8, with strength 0.4 for brightness and contrast, 0.2 for saturation and 0.1 for hue; a strength s multiplies the brightness, contrast or saturation by a random factor from 1 − s to 1 + s;
  • turn grey with probability 0.2;
  • Gaussian blur with probability 1.0 for the first global crop, 0.1 for the second and 0.5 for each local crop, at a random radius of 0.1 to 2.0 pixels;
  • solarise, which keeps every value below 128 and turns every value v of 128 or more into 255 − v, only in the second global crop and with probability 0.2.

Now the split. In DINO all crops pass through the student while only the 2 global crops pass through the teacher, which, the authors write, encourages local-to-global correspondences. SelfPatch changes the split, as Part 4 explains: its teacher processes all 10 crops.Fact

What does that ask of the student? A local crop of 96 pixels holds 6 × 6 = 36 patches against the 196 of a global crop, often a corner of the scene. From that corner the student must produce what the teacher says about a view that covers most of the picture. Describing the part in a way that agrees with the whole is a strong hint about what the whole contains.Toy

Lab 3A draws one random set of crops on a picture: the drawn sample, your own image, or one of the course's photographs. Press 'New crops' a few times, switch the changes on, then switch between DINO and SelfPatch to see which crops the teacher gets.Toy

Decision

Cut every image into two global crops of 224 pixels and several local crops of 96 pixels: in SelfPatch's code and our runs the global crops cover 0.25 to 1.0 of the area and 8 local crops cover 0.05 to 0.25; the released DINO run used 10 local crops.

Why

All crops pass through the student and only the global views through the teacher, which, the authors write, encourages local-to-global correspondences.Fact

Evidence

Without multi-crop, k-NN accuracy falls from 72.8 % to 67.9 % (Table 7, row 4). In the supplement, two global and ten local crops reach 74.6 % linear accuracy after 24.2 hours of training where two global crops alone reach 72.5 % after 45.9 hours, at a peak memory of 15.4 GB per GPU against 9.3 GB.Fact

What it does to training

Each image gives 18 loss pairs, 16 of which ask a small crop for a large crop's answer, and a 96-pixel crop holds 36 patches against 196, so the extra views cost far less than extra global crops would.Toy

If you changed it

The supplement moves the scale s that separates local from global crops: 0.08 gives 65.6 % and 0.32 gives 69.8 % k-NN accuracy, best near 0.3. Adding 2, 6 and 10 local crops raises the linear accuracy each time, by less from 6 to 10 at 300 epochs.Fact

Decision

Change every crop at random: mirror it, jitter its colours, turn it grey, blur it, and solarise the second global crop, each with a fixed probability.

Why

DINO follows BYOL's augmentations, colour jittering, Gaussian blur and solarisation, together with multi-crop.Fact

Evidence

The probabilities of the official code: mirror 0.5, colour jitter 0.8, greyscale 0.2, blur 1.0 for the first global crop, 0.1 for the second and 0.5 for each local crop, and solarise 0.2 for the second global crop only.Fact

What it does to training

Two views of one image now differ in colour, sharpness and brightness as well as in framing, so an answer that rests on colour alone cannot make them agree; an answer that describes what the image shows can.Toy

If you changed it

Without the changes, two views of one picture differ only in their framing. Switch them on and off in Lab 3A and compare the small pictures.Toy

3.3 The head and the prototypes

Assumes: 3.1, a layer and GELU (Part 1), the class token (Part 2). Gives: how a crop becomes scores over 65,536 prototypes, and why a head sits between the backbone and the loss.

The student and the teacher must each give their answer for a crop in a form that can be compared. The projection head produces it. In DINO the output of the class token feeds the head, and the head exists only for training: the features that later tasks use are the backbone's output.Fact

The head is a three-layer MLP. It maps the class token's 384 numbers to 2,048, then to 2,048 again, then to a 256-number bottleneck, with GELUs in between; this MLP alone has 5,509,376 parameters. The bottleneck vector is then L2-normalised, scaled to length 1, so that it lies on the surface of a sphere. A last weight-normalised layer, which stores each of its weight vectors as a direction and a length, compares it with 65,536 learned directions, the prototypes, and gives one score per prototype.Fact

In SelfPatch's code, and so in our runs, every prototype also has length 1, so each score is the cosine between the crop's vector and that prototype, a number from −1 to 1; the released DINO run let those lengths be learned.Fact

One picture of the prototypes, as an analogy only: 65,536 categories that the network invents for itself, and the student must sort one crop into the same categories as the teacher sorts another crop of the same picture.Toy

The course's real models are SelfPatch-family networks, and their backbone has no class token: a 224-pixel crop leaves it as 196 tokens of 384 numbers. Converting DINO's backbone for that network drops 1,536 parameters (the class token, its position row and the final LayerNorm), so the converted network no longer computes DINO's own forward pass. In those networks the class output that feeds this head comes from SelfPatch's aggregation head, its own class token attending over the patch tokens (Part 4).Fact

Decision

Put a projection head between the backbone and the loss: a three-layer MLP from 384 to 2,048 to 2,048 to a 256-number bottleneck, L2 normalisation, then a weight-normalised layer of K = 65,536 outputs, and no batch normalisation anywhere.

Why

In the supplement's words, as in other self-supervised frameworks, a projection head improves the accuracy greatly, while the features used downstream are the backbone's; the design is inspired by the projection head with a prototype layer of SwAV, and it has no batch normalisation.Fact

Evidence

With the L2 bottleneck, 2, 3 and 4 linear layers give 62.2, 68.0 and 69.3 % k-NN accuracy; without it, 1 and 2 layers give 61.6 and 62.9 % and 3 or 4 layers fail at 0.1 %. GELU against ReLU: 69.7 against 68.9 %; batch normalisation in the heads: 68.6 against 69.7 % without.Fact

What it does to training

The head turns a crop's summary into the scores the loss compares, and the gradient of the loss flows back through the head into the backbone. Whatever is peculiar to the training task can settle in the head, while the backbone's tokens, which later tasks and our decoder read, stay general.Toy

If you changed it

Without the L2 bottleneck, a head of three or four linear layers does not train (0.1 %): the bottleneck is what keeps a deep head stable.Fact

Decision

Score each crop against K = 65,536 learned prototypes.

Why

The supplement finds that a large output dimension improves the accuracy, and that the L2-normalisation bottleneck keeps the number of parameters moderate.Fact

Evidence

k-NN accuracy for K = 1,024: 67.8 %; 4,096: 69.3 %; 16,384: 69.2 %; 65,536: 69.7 %; 262,144: 69.1 %.Fact

What it does to training

Many prototypes give many soft categories, so different images can claim different ones, and the student is pulled towards the teacher's categories image by image.Toy

If you changed it

Fewer prototypes cost accuracy (67.8 % at 1,024) and more did not help (69.1 % at 262,144). SelfPatch's patch head uses 4,096 (Part 4).Fact

3.4 Centring and sharpening

Assumes: 3.3 and softmax with a temperature (Part 1). Gives: the teacher's distribution, the two forms of collapse, and how two opposite pushes avoid both.

Scores become probabilities through a softmax after division by a temperature τ, and a smaller τ makes the result sharper, so the top score takes more of the probability. The result is a probability distribution: one number per prototype, none negative, all adding up to one.Fact

The student divides its scores by τs = 0.1. SelfPatch's code and our runs divide the teacher's by τt = 0.04 for the whole run, so the teacher's distribution is sharper than the student's; DINO itself raises the teacher's temperature from 0.04 to 0.07 during the first 30 epochs.Fact

Nothing so far says what the right answer is, so the two networks could agree in a useless way, by giving every picture the same answer. The authors watch two numbers. The teacher's entropy, a measure of how spread out its distribution is, goes to 0 when one prototype dominates and to its largest value, ln K, when every prototype gets the same share. The KL divergence from the teacher to the student, a distance between the two distributions that is 0 when they are equal (3.5 works one out), goes to 0 in both cases: a KL of zero means a constant output.Fact

DINO's answer to both forms is a pair of opposite pushes. Centring keeps a running average of the teacher's scores, the centre, and subtracts it from the teacher's scores before the softmax; after every step the centre moves a tenth of the way towards the batch's average, c ← 0.9 c + 0.1 × (batch mean of the teacher's outputs). Sharpening is the low teacher temperature. Centring stops one prototype from dominating yet pushes towards a uniform output, sharpening pushes the other way, and the authors find that using both together avoids collapse with a momentum teacher.Fact

In DINO the centre averages the teacher's outputs for the 2 global crops; SelfPatch's code averages the image-level outputs of all 10 teacher crops.Fact

Collapse, and how DINO avoids it

What happens to the answers when centring or sharpening is switched off?

Toy

What you are looking at: a film of 300 training steps of a toy student and teacher on four pictures, A to D: each picture's answer as a point of the triangle of three prototypes (blue dots for the teacher, green rings for the student, faint lines for the teacher's path), and the teacher's entropy and its KL divergence to the student over the steps, the two numbers of DINO's collapse study.Toy

Real model versus this toy: the toy keeps DINO's rules (the teacher a moving average of the student, the centre a moving average of the teacher's scores with rate 0.9, temperatures 0.04 and 0.1, two noisy views per picture, the cross-entropy, stop-gradient) and shrinks everything else: four pictures of two numbers each, a network of nine weights, three prototypes, and a teacher that moves at 0.98 per step, far faster than the real 0.996 to 1. Decision

Subtract a centre c, a moving average of the teacher's scores over batches with rate m = 0.9, from the teacher's scores before its softmax.

Why

Centring prevents one dimension from dominating, while it encourages a uniform output. It depends only on first-order batch statistics and acts like a bias term added to the teacher, and because the centre moves as a moving average, DINO works across batch sizes.Fact

Evidence

In the collapse study, without centring the teacher's entropy falls to 0 while its KL divergence to the student falls to 0: a collapse. The centre's rate matters: 0, 0.9 and 0.99 give 69.1, 69.7 and 69.4 % k-NN accuracy, while 0.999, too slow, collapses to 0.1 %.Fact

What it does to training

A prototype that scores high for every picture raises the centre by the same amount and loses its lead, so the targets differ from picture to picture and the student has to look at each picture to match them.Toy

If you changed it

Switch centring off in Lab 3E: at the lab's default start the four pictures' answers run into one corner of the triangle, the first form of collapse.Toy

Decision

Give the teacher a low temperature, τt = 0.04 rising to 0.07 over the first 30 epochs in DINO (0.04 for the whole run in SelfPatch's code and our runs), against τs = 0.1 for the student.

Why

Sharpening has the opposite effect to centring: it pushes towards one dominant dimension. Applying both operations balances their effects, which the authors find sufficient to avoid collapse in the presence of a momentum teacher.Fact

Evidence

k-NN accuracy for a teacher temperature of 0: 43.9 %; 0.02: 66.7 %; 0.04: 69.6 %; 0.06: 68.7 %; 0.08: 0.1 %, a collapse; 0.04 rising to 0.07: 69.7 %. Above 0.06, training collapses unless the temperature starts lower and warms up.Fact

What it does to training

A target sharper than the student's own answer asks the student to commit: the gradient (Ps − Pt) / τs pushes hardest on the prototypes the teacher favours, as the walk of 3.4 shows.Toy

If you changed it

Without sharpening, the teacher's entropy rises to its largest value, −log(1/K), and its KL divergence to the student goes to 0: the second form of collapse. In Lab 3E the answers drift to the middle of the triangle.Fact

3.5 The loss over the pairs of crops

Assumes: 3.4, a logarithm and a gradient (Part 1). Gives: the cross-entropy, worked with five prototypes; the 18 pairs of one image; and what one step of the gradient does to the student.

Now the two networks can be compared. For one pair of crops the teacher gives a probability distribution over the prototypes, and so does the student. DINO trains the student to match the teacher by minimising the cross-entropy H(teacher, student) = −Σ teacher × log(student) with respect to the student's weights.Fact

The teacher's probabilities are held fixed while the gradient is computed, a step called stop-gradient, so only the student learns from the loss.Fact

One image gives several pairs. The teacher sees 2 crops and the student 10, and a crop is never compared with itself, so the loss averages 2 × 9 = 18 pairs; with the 10 local crops of the released DINO run the same rule gives 2 × 11 = 22 pairs. DINO's Eq. (3) writes it as a sum over each global view x seen by the teacher and every view x′ ≠ x seen by the student.Fact

Decision

Match the student's distribution to the teacher's by minimising the cross-entropy H(Pt, Ps) = −Σ Pt log Ps.

Why

DINO presents its objective as knowledge distillation: given a fixed teacher, the student learns to match the teacher's distributions by minimising the cross-entropy with respect to its own weights.Fact

Evidence

With a mean squared error in its place, k-NN accuracy falls from 72.8 % to 52.6 % and linear accuracy from 76.1 % to 62.4 % (Table 7, row 5).Fact

What it does to training

The gradient on each student score is (Ps − Pt) / τs: the scores of prototypes the teacher favours more than the student does go up, the others go down, and the push stops when the two distributions agree.Toy

If you changed it

SelfPatch's paper writes the same distance as a KL divergence; the two differ by the teacher's entropy, which has no gradient, so they train the student identically.Fact

Decision

Use no negatives: no contrastive loss and no queue of other images; the student only matches the teacher's distribution for views of the same image.

Why

The momentum encoder was first introduced as a substitute for a queue in contrastive learning. In DINO its role differs, because there is neither a queue nor a contrastive loss: centring and sharpening of the momentum teacher's outputs are enough to avoid collapse.Fact

Evidence

On the same ViT-S/16, MoCo-v2 with its InfoNCE loss reaches 62.0 % k-NN and 71.6 % linear accuracy without multi-crop (Table 7, row 8), against DINO's 72.8 % and 76.1 %; with the same multi-crop it reaches 65.4 % and 73.4 % against 72.7 % and 75.9 %.Fact

What it does to training

Each image is compared only with its own views, never with another image of the batch, and the supplement trains with batches from 128 to 1,024 images and finds that small batches work almost as well.Fact

If you changed it

Our rule (Part 5) does add negatives, at the level of patches: in each crop, the tokens least similar to an anchor.Fact

Decision

3.6 The teacher is an average of the student's past

Assumes: 3.1 and 3.5. Gives: the moving average, its cosine schedule, and how much of the starting network the teacher still holds after an epoch.

After every step, each of the teacher's weights moves a small step towards the matching weight of the student: teacher ← m × teacher + (1 − m) × student. The number m is the momentum. In DINO it follows a cosine schedule from 0.996 up to 1 during training; our runs go from 0.9995 up to 1. SelfPatch's official default is 0.996, and the note it prints about that setting recommends 0.9995 for a batch of 256.Fact

Roughly, the teacher averages the last 1 / (1 − m) steps: 2,000 steps at m = 0.9995 and 250 at m = 0.996.Fact

The teacher's moving average and its cosine schedule

θt ← λ θt + (1 − λ) θs

λ(k) = 1 − (1 − λ0) · (cos(π k / n) + 1) / 2

The teacher receives no gradient: the moving average is its only update. Each step moves every teacher weight by (1 − λ)(θs − θt), a fixed fraction of its distance to the student's weight, so the teacher follows the student's path smoothed over roughly the last 1 / (1 − λ) steps. The student moves by the gradient; the teacher moves by the student.Fact

Decision

Build the teacher as an exponential moving average of the student's weights, θt ← λ θt + (1 − λ) θs, after every step.

Why

The authors find this momentum encoder particularly well suited: it performs a form of model ensembling similar to Polyak-Ruppert averaging, and the teacher performs better than the student throughout training, so it guides the student with targets of higher quality.Fact

Evidence

The supplement compares teachers: a copy of the student 0.1 %, the student of the previous iteration 0.1 %, the student of the previous epoch 66.6 % and the momentum teacher 72.8 % k-NN accuracy.Fact

What it does to training

The targets change slowly: at λ = 0.9995 the teacher is roughly an average of the last 2,000 steps, so one unusual batch cannot drag the targets around.Fact

If you changed it

Copying the student fails to converge, while freezing the teacher for an epoch works surprisingly well. Compare DINO's 0.996 with our 0.9995 in Lab 3C.Fact

Decision

Raise the momentum λ along a cosine from 0.996 to 1 during training (0.9995 to 1 in our runs).

Why

Evidence

Our runs' telemetry: λ = 0.9995 at step 1, 0.999503 at step 463, 0.99975 at step 4,620 and 1.0 at step 9,240.Real

3.7 What DINO learns

Assumes: 3.1 to 3.6 and attention (Part 2). Gives: what the trained network's attention shows, and why a decoder that never sees a label can read objects out of its keys.

What does all this produce? The DINO authors report that the features of self-supervised ViTs contain explicit information about the segmentation of an image, which pixels belong to which object, and that this information does not emerge as clearly with supervised ViTs or with convolutional networks.Fact

They checked this against outlines drawn by people. Masks made by keeping 60 % of a ViT-S/16's attention mass overlap the object masks of the PASCAL VOC validation pictures with a Jaccard similarity of 45.9 for DINO, 27.3 for a supervised ViT and 22.0 for random weights, a measurement made with human masks after training.Fact

Labels also measure the features with the yardstick of 3.1: on ImageNet, a k-NN classifier fitted with labels on DINO's ViT-S/16 features names the right class first for 74.5 % of pictures, and a linear classifier for 77.0 %, both on frozen features that training no longer changes.Fact

Two object finders that work without labels, MaskCut and TokenCut, read one particular part of the last block: the keys of its attention, which they use as patch features. The thesis's label-free decoder does the same: at 480 pixels it takes 900 × 384 numbers from the teacher's last block, one key of 384 numbers for each of the 900 patches, subtracts the picture's mean key from every key, and links two patches when the cosine of their keys is above 0.2, giving every other pair a tiny weight instead (Part 6).Fact

The idea fits in three moves, which the figure draws. Link two patches when their keys are similar. If the patches of an object have similar keys and differ from the background, the links form a dense island over the object. A cut then splits the patches into two groups so that few links cross between the groups, and one group becomes the object's mask. Part 6 builds the decoder in full and runs it on real keys.Toy

Decision

Read the scene's objects from what DINO's training leaves in the last block, its attention and its keys, with no label.

Why

The authors report that self-supervised ViT features contain explicit information about the segmentation of an image, which does not emerge as clearly with supervised ViTs or with convolutional networks.Fact

Evidence

Masks that keep 60 % of a ViT-S/16's attention mass overlap the object masks of the PASCAL VOC validation pictures with a Jaccard similarity of 45.9 for DINO, 27.3 for a supervised ViT and 22.0 for random weights, measured with human masks after training.Fact

What it does to training

Nothing in the loss names an object. One reading, the course's own: to give one answer for a small crop and a large crop of the same picture, the network has to find what the crops share, and that is usually an object.Toy

If you changed it

The supplement measures the overlap for other self-supervised training: 45.1 for DINO without multi-crop, 46.3 for MoCo-v2, 47.8 for BYOL and 46.8 for SwAV, against 27.3 for supervised training, and the authors observe the scene layout and object boundaries across these methods. Glossary of this page

AdamW
The optimiser DINO uses. It scales each weight's step by the recent history of that weight's gradients, and it applies weight decay as a separate small pull towards zero.
Augmentation
A random change applied to a training crop, such as a mirror flip, a colour change or a blur, so that two views of one image differ.
Backpropagation
The procedure that computes the gradient for every weight by passing the effect of the loss backwards through the network, layer by layer.
Batch
The pictures used together in one step of training.
Bottleneck
The narrow layer of 256 numbers at the end of the projection head's MLP, just before the L2 normalisation.
Centre
In DINO, a running average of the teacher's scores, one number per prototype, updated after every step.
Centring
Subtracting the centre from the teacher's scores before the softmax, so that no prototype can win for every picture.
Collapse
A useless outcome of training in which the networks give every picture the same answer: one prototype takes all the probability, or the probability spreads evenly over all of them.
Colour jitter
An augmentation that changes a crop's brightness, contrast, saturation and hue by random amounts.
Cosine schedule
A setting that moves from a start value to an end value along half a cosine wave: slowly at first, fastest in the middle and slowly at the end.
Crop
A rectangle cut out of a picture and resized. In training the networks receive crops of the photographs.
Cross-entropy
DINO's loss for one pair of crops: minus the sum, over the prototypes, of the teacher's probability times the logarithm of the student's probability. It is smallest when the student's probabilities equal the teacher's.
Distillation
Training a student network to reproduce a teacher network's outputs.
Entropy
A measure of how spread out a probability distribution is: zero when one outcome takes all the probability, largest, the logarithm of the number of outcomes, when all of them are equally likely.
Epoch
One pass over all the training pictures.
Exponential moving average (EMA)
A running average in which each new value gets a small weight and older values fade away step by step. DINO's teacher is an exponential moving average of the student's weights.
Gaussian blur
An augmentation that softens a crop by averaging each pixel with its neighbours, the nearer ones weighing more.
Global crop
One of the two large crops taken from each image at every step. In SelfPatch's code and our runs it covers 0.25 to 1.0 of the image's area and is resized to 224 × 224 pixels.
Gradient
For every weight, how the loss would change if that weight moved a little. Training moves each weight in the direction that lowers the loss.
Gradient clipping
Shrinking any gradient that grows past a set size, so that one step cannot jump too far.
InfoNCE
A contrastive loss: a view must pick out its partner among negatives, scored by the share that their similarity takes in a softmax over the partner and the negatives. MoCo-v2 uses it between images; our rule uses it between the patches of one crop (Part 5).
Jaccard similarity
The area two masks share divided by the area they cover together: one for identical masks and zero for masks that do not overlap.
k-NN classifier
A simple test of features with labels: a picture gets the label that wins a vote among the k labelled pictures whose features lie closest to its own. DINO weights the vote and lets the 20 nearest take part.
Key
In attention, the vector a token offers to be matched against the other tokens' queries. MaskCut, TokenCut and the thesis's decoder read the last block's keys as patch features.
KL divergence
Cross-entropy minus the teacher's entropy: a distance between two probability distributions that is zero when they are equal.
L2 normalisation
Dividing a vector by its length so that its length becomes one; the vector then lies on the surface of a sphere.
Label-free decoder
A fixed procedure that turns a network's features into object masks without using any labels. The thesis's decoder reads the teacher's last-block keys.
Learning rate
The size of each step of training, set by a schedule over the run.
Linear classifier
A single linear map from features to class scores, fitted with labels: a common test of what the features already contain.
Local crop
One of the small crops taken from each image at every step, 8 of them in SelfPatch's code and our runs. Each covers 0.05 to 0.25 of the image's area and is resized to 96 × 96 pixels.
MaskCut
A label-free object finder that links patches with similar keys and splits the resulting graph with normalised cuts, one object at a time.
Momentum
The number m in the teacher's update, just below one, which sets how slowly the teacher follows the student. The centre's rate is the same kind of number: the larger it is, the slower the centre moves.
Multi-crop
DINO's way of making views: a few large global crops and several small local crops of the same image.
Nat
The unit of entropy and cross-entropy when the logarithm is the natural one.
Probability distribution
A list of numbers, one per possible outcome, none negative and all adding up to one.
Projection head
The small network after the backbone that turns a crop's summary into scores over the prototypes. It exists for training; later tasks read the backbone.
Prototype
One of the learned directions that the projection head compares its output with; DINO's head has 65,536 of them.
Segmentation
Dividing a picture into regions, for example one region per object.
Self-distillation
Distillation in which the teacher is built from the student itself: in DINO, as a moving average of the student's past weights.
Sharpening
Using a low teacher temperature so that the teacher's probabilities clearly favour a few prototypes.
Solarise
An augmentation that keeps every pixel value below 128 and turns every value v of 128 or more into 255 − v, so the bright parts turn dark.
Step
One round of training: compute the loss on a batch, compute the gradient, and move the weights one small step. Also called an update.
Stochastic depth
A training trick in which the student randomly skips a block's residual branches, its attention and MLP steps, for some pictures.
Stop-gradient
Holding a quantity fixed while the gradient is computed, so that no learning flows through it; DINO applies it to the teacher's outputs.
Student
The network that learns from the loss.
Supervised learning
Training that uses labels to say what each output should be.
Teacher
The network whose outputs the student learns to match. In DINO it is an average of the student's past weights and receives no gradient.
Temperature
A number τ that divides the scores before a softmax: a smaller τ gives a sharper distribution, a larger τ a flatter one.
View
One changed version of a training image: a crop with its augmentations.
Warm-up
A period at the start of training in which the learning rate rises gradually to its peak.
Weight decay
A small pull of every weight towards zero at each step, which keeps weights from growing large. A bigger weight-decay number means a stronger pull.
